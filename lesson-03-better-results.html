Getting
better results

A disappointing answer is a diagnosable fault, not a verdict on the tool. Below: the five faults you'll actually hit, the habit that prevents most of them, the four competencies underneath all of it, and a way to test whether Claude is any good at your work.

Part 01

The fault chart

Five things that go wrong, what's causing each one, and the sentence that fixes it. Read the symptom line first — that's the part you'll recognise from your own chats.

01

The answer is too generic

Symptom

It reads like it could have been written for anyone. Nothing in it is about your situation.

Cause

Your prompt didn't carry enough context about your specific situation.

The fix

Add your audience, your role and your constraints. Name who's reading it and what they already know.

Instead of Write an email about the project delay
Try Write an email to our enterprise client explaining that the software integration will be delayed by two weeks. They've been patient so far but this is the second delay. Keep it professional but apologetic.

What actually changed: the second version names the reader, the specific fact, the history, and the tone. Four pieces of information you already had — you just hadn't written them down.

02

The response is too long, or too short

Symptom

Six paragraphs when you wanted three lines. Or three lines when you needed the full analysis.

Cause

Claude is guessing at the right length, because nothing in the prompt told it.

The fix
  • "Give me a two-paragraph summary."
  • "Keep this under 100 words."
  • "I need a comprehensive analysis — length isn't a concern."
03

It didn't follow my format

Symptom

Right content, wrong shape. Prose where you wanted a table; a wall of text where you wanted headers.

Cause

Claude understood what you want, but not how you want it presented.

The fix

Show, don't just tell. Paste an example of the format you want — or describe the structure outright: "Use bullet points with bold headers for each section."

04

Confident-sounding information that was wrong

Don't skim this one
Symptom

A fact, figure or citation delivered with total assurance — that turns out to be false.

Cause

Claude can generate plausible but incorrect information, and it happens most on specific facts and niche topics.

The fix
  • For high-stakes work, verify key facts independently.
  • Ask Claude to cite sources, or to say how confident it is.
  • Turn on web search so answers are grounded in current information.

Keep this: fluent prose is not evidence. The more specific and the more niche the claim, the harder you should check it.

05

The tone isn't right

Symptom

Technically correct, socially wrong. Too stiff for a teammate, too breezy for a board.

Cause

Claude defaults to helpful and professional, which won't match every situation you're in.

The fix

Describe the tone in plain language — "Make this more conversational," or "This should sound authoritative and formal." Better still, paste a paragraph written in the voice you're after.

Part 02

The iteration mindset

Your first prompt rarely produces a perfect result — and that isn't a failure state, it's how this is meant to work. The opening prompt starts a conversation; it isn't a one-shot request.

Habit 01

First drafts are starting points

Read what came back and name it: what's working, what isn't. Then refine. Judging beats rewriting from scratch.

Habit 02

Give specific feedback

"Make it shorter" works. "Cut the first two paragraphs and make the conclusion more action-oriented" works far better — because it names the edit, not the mood.

Habit 03

Know when to start fresh

If a conversation has gone off track, a new chat with a clearer prompt is often faster than steering the old one back.

Part 03

AI Fluency: the four D's

AI Fluency is the ability to collaborate well with AI — not knowing which buttons to click, but having the judgement to use it well across different situations. Four competencies, and the point is that they work combined.

AI
Fluency
Competency 01

Delegation

Deciding what the human should do, what the AI should do, and how to split the work between them. It rests on knowing your own goals and knowing what AI can actually do — then choosing deliberately.

Competency 02

Description

Communicating well with the system: saying clearly what output you want, guiding how it gets there, and specifying the behaviour you expect.

You practised this in Lesson 02
Competency 03

Discernment

Evaluating what comes back — critically, not passively. Judging quality, accuracy and appropriateness, and working out where it needs to improve.

You practised this in the fault chart
Competency 04

Diligence

Using AI responsibly and ethically: choosing thoughtfully, staying transparent about where AI was involved, and owning the result of AI-assisted work.

Fault 04 is this competency

Where this comes from: the 4D Framework for AI Fluency was developed through research collaboration between Prof. Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design) and Prof. Joseph Feller (University College Cork). Anthropic publishes a free AI Fluency course covering all four competencies in depth.

Part 04

The eval loop

"Is Claude actually good at this?" is a question you can answer rather than guess at. Evals are a systematic way to test how Claude performs on the tasks that matter to you — and you need no infrastructure at all to run one.

Why bother
  • Find where Claude adds the most value in your particular workflow.
  • Spot the tasks where you'll need to supply more context or examples.
  • Build justified confidence in the outputs you lean on repeatedly.
Step 01 · once

Gather examples

Collect 5–10 examples of a task you do regularly: emails you've written, reports you've built, analyses you've run.

Step 02 · loop

Create test prompts

Write prompts that would produce similar outputs — and include the context you'd naturally have when doing this work for real.

Step 03 · loop

Compare outputs

Run them, then hold Claude's answers next to your own and ask:

  • Does it capture the key information?
  • Is the tone and style appropriate?
  • What's missing, or could be better?
Step 04 · loop

Refine your approach

Adjust the prompts, add examples showing what good looks like, or mark the places where human review is non-negotiable. Then go back to step 02.

Read the shape, not just the steps: step 01 happens once. Steps 02–04 repeat. The loop is the eval — running it a single time tells you almost nothing.
