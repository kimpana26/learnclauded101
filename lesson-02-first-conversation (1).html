Your first
conversation

Claude brings the intelligence. You bring the context and the expertise that makes the work mean anything. Six branches below, and they're not steps — you'll use them in whatever order the work demands.

One conversation, six branches. Colours match the cards.

01 Mindset

Talk to it like a coworker

PurposeSettle who is bringing what, before you type anything at all.

Do this
  • Write naturally, concisely, conversationally — the way you'd brief a colleague, not the way you'd query a search engine.
  • Bring the context and the expertise. That half is yours and nothing replaces it.
  • Start anywhere on the range: a quick question like brainstorming code names, or a full request to co-create a file.
Most people miss
  • Claude amplifies what you can do — it doesn't replace your judgement. The intelligence is Claude's; the meaning is yours.
  • The real power shows up in continued, frequent conversation, not in one perfect prompt.

The shift that matters: you're not querying a database. You're briefing a collaborator who wasn't in the room.

02 The prompt

Three moves make a brief

PurposeTurn a request into something anyone could actually act on.

Do this
  • 1 · Set the stage. What's your role, what's the objective, what should Claude know about your work?
  • 2 · Define the task. What action do you want — write, analyse, build, something else?
  • 3 · Specify rules. What style and tone? Any example you can attach to show what good looks like?
Most people miss
  • "Examples" belongs in rules. Attaching one sample of what you want beats a paragraph describing it.
  • The stage is the piece people skip — and it's the piece that makes the answer yours instead of generic.
  • The task line should name a verb. "About Q3" isn't a task; "summarise Q3 for a board audience" is.

The same prompt, cut into its three jobs

Set the stage I'm the marketing lead at an indie streaming startup, and we're preparing an investor pitch deck for Series A investors.
Define the task Can you research the current state of the independent film streaming market and identify key trends, competitor positioning, and growth opportunities?
Specify rules Use current web research with citations and structure it as a professional report of up to 5 pages, with an executive summary, market analysis, competitive landscape, and growth opportunities.

Read the three rows top to bottom and you have the original prompt, word for word. Nothing clever was added — the clauses were already doing three different jobs.

Where this comes from: the framework is adapted from the 4D Framework for AI Fluency — Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence — developed by Prof. Rick Dakan (Ringling College of Art and Design) and Prof. Joseph Feller (University College Cork).

The test: read your prompt back as if you were a new hire. If you couldn't start work from it, neither can Claude.

03 Context

Hand over the material

PurposeShow Claude the thing instead of describing the thing.

Do this
  • Upload a document → "summarise the key points."
  • Share an image → "describe what you see here."
  • Attach a spreadsheet → "what trends are in this data?"
  • Upload code → "explain how this works" or "find the bug."
Most people miss
  • Claude reads the visual parts too — charts and graphics inside a PDF, not only its text.
  • Supported types include PDF, DOCX, CSV, TXT and common images like PNG and JPEG.
  • Settings → General → "What personal preferences should Claude consider?" applies to every conversation you have, forever.
  • Connectors pull in context from tools you already work in.

Why it works: an upload is a shortcut past three rounds of you explaining what you meant.

04 Iterate

The first reply is a draft

PurposeSteer the conversation instead of restarting your thinking.

Do this — three moves
  • Ask a follow-up. "Expand on the second point." "That's helpful — make it more concise."
  • Give feedback. "This is good, but the tone is too formal. Make it more conversational."
  • Redirect, or restart. "Actually, I was asking about X, not Y." Worst case, open a new chat to fully refresh the context.
Most people miss
  • The pencil icon on your own message. Edit and resubmit the prompt rather than stacking a correction on top of it.
  • Chaining bite-sized prompts beats one enormous one — you get to steer based on what actually came back.

The habit: small prompts, chained. That's a dialogue. One-shot prompting is a lottery ticket.

05 Personalise

Set it once, not every time

PurposeStop re-explaining yourself at the top of every new chat.

Do this
  • Memory saves key context from your conversations — your role, preferences, past decisions, working style — and carries it into new chats.
  • Styles control how Claude writes. Pick a preset — concise, formal, explanatory — or describe your own.
Most people miss
  • You stay in control: review, edit or delete anything Claude remembers, any time, in Settings. It syncs across your devices.
  • A custom Style is just a plain-English description of how you want Claude to write. No special syntax.
  • Once set, a Style applies across all your conversations automatically.

The compounding part: everything on this branch pays out on every future conversation, not just this one.

06 Get inspired

Borrow, don't invent

PurposeSteal a worked example from someone with your job instead of starting at a blank box.

Do this
  • Open the Use Case Gallery — practical examples across research, writing, coding, analysis and everyday work, solo or with a team.
  • Filter by Category to find your job: EducationFinanceHRLegalLife SciencesMarketingNonprofitsPersonalProfessionalResearchSalesCoworkCustom visuals
  • Filter by Feature to learn one capability at a time: ArtifactsConnectorsExtended ThinkingProjectsResearchSkillsWeb Search
  • Filter by Product if you want examples in one surface: Claude.aiClaude CoworkClaude in ExcelClaude DesignClaude in Chrome
Most people miss
  • There are six pages, not one. Page one leans towards education, nonprofits and finance — filter rather than scroll and conclude nothing fits.
  • Every card lists the model and the features the example used, so you know what to switch on before you start.
  • Further down the page sit ready-made starter prompts grouped under Write, Learn and Code — copy one and swap in your details.
  • Custom visuals is a whole category where Claude draws the thing inside the chat as you talk. Most people never try it.

Six real examples, to show the shape

Plan your syllabus, and see which weeks are actually locked by prerequisitesEducation
Map a lit review mid-conversation to surface the underlying debateEducation
Put three budget futures side by side and see where the pressure landsNonprofits
Update a financial model after earnings, then build the deck pageFinance
Audit a folder of visual assets against your brand guidelinesCowork
Turn one textbook spread into handouts at three reading levelsCowork

Read the starter prompts like a worked answer to branch 02. Each one asks Claude to put 1–2 clarifying questions back to you, invites you to attach documents, permits web search and connectors, fixes the tone, and names the output format. That is set the stage, define the task, specify rules — written out by the people who built it.

The shortcut: the fastest way to get good at prompting is to read a good prompt for a job like yours, then swap in your own details.
