The ones worth
ten minutes

The other library fixes prompts you're about to type. These are for when you have a real situation and time to think. Each one is long on purpose — you fill in the highlighted slots and it does work you'd otherwise do badly, or not at all.

Every prompt here has the same four parts — learn the shape, not the fifty
  1. A role. "You are a negotiation strategist" changes the vocabulary, the depth and the assumptions.
  2. Context slots. The facts only you have. This is where the quality comes from.
  3. Numbered deliverables. Exactly what you want back, as a list it can't skip.
  4. A rules block ending with "end with the one thing…" — which forces a conclusion rather than a summary.
Read this before you copy anything

Dissect one before you paste it

A prompt you paste without understanding is a spell. A prompt you can take apart is a skill. Before you use any of these, find its three parts — they're in every single one:

  1. The role — one line at the top that stops it answering as a generic assistant.
  2. Your context — the bracketed fields. This is the half no prompt can supply for you.
  3. The rules — almost always including one that forbids the lazy answer: don't be polite, don't tell me it reads fine, don't summarise.

If you can name those three in a prompt you've never seen, you don't need this page any more — which is the point of it. Lesson 02 is where the three come from.

Before the room

01–08 · run these the day before, not the hour after
1Before the room

Map a difficult conversation before it happens

You've rehearsed it and it always goes your way. Then they say something you didn't plan for.

Prompt

You are an expert in communication and conflict resolution. The situation: [what happened and why this conversation is happening] The person: [their role, how they typically react under pressure] What I need to walk away with: [your actual goal] Map this as a decision tree: 1. The three most likely opening responses they'll give — realistic ones, not optimistic ones 2. My best counter to each, under two sentences 3. The trap I'm most likely to fall into, and how to avoid it 4. The exact moment this is most likely to go sideways Rules: - Be specific to this situation, not general advice about difficult conversations - End with the one sentence I should have ready if it goes badly

2Before the room

Know every objection before you walk in

You know your material and still get blindsided by one question.

Prompt

You are a senior advisor who has watched hundreds of pitches fail. My proposal: [what you're proposing] The room: [who's there, their roles, what they care about, what they're sceptical of] What I need them to agree to: [the specific outcome] Generate the seven most likely objections this specific audience will raise. For each: 1. State it in the words they'd actually use 2. Give me the sharpest answer, under three sentences 3. Say whether it's a real concern or a reflex Rules: - Order them by how likely they are, not by how easy they are to answer - Include at least one objection I probably haven't considered - End with the single objection that would most likely kill this, and what to do about it in advance

3Before the room

Profile the other side of a negotiation

You know your position well and theirs almost not at all, so you improvise and concede.

Prompt

You are a negotiation strategist who specialises in understanding the other side. The negotiation: [salary / contract / price / terms] The other side: [their role, organisation, what you know about their situation] My position: [what you want, what you'd accept, your walk-away] Context: [history, timing, market conditions] Build a profile of them: 1. Their most likely real priority here 2. A constraint they're operating under that they probably won't mention 3. Where they have room to move and where they genuinely don't 4. The argument most likely to move them 5. The move that would make them dig in Rules: - Specific to this situation, not generic negotiation theory - Include what they need to be able to say yes without losing face - End with the single most important thing to know before I walk in

4Before the room

Walk into a professional meeting as an equal

Doctors, lawyers, advisors, contractors — they control the conversation because they control the information.

Prompt

You are a senior [specialist in that field] preparing a colleague for a meeting with another professional in your field. The meeting: [what it's about — what you'll discuss, decide or be told] My situation: [what you know, what you don't, what worries you] What I need to walk away with: [the decision or information you need] Prepare me: 1. The five questions an informed person asks in this situation 2. The red flags that should make me ask for a second opinion 3. The thing professionals in this field rarely volunteer unless asked 4. The moment I should push back, and the words to use Rules: - Assume I know nothing about the field - Be specific to my situation - End with what most people leave this kind of meeting still not knowing

5Before the room

Interview rehearsal with real feedback

A list of likely questions teaches you nothing. Answering badly once teaches you a lot.

Prompt

You are an experienced hiring manager for [role] at [type of company]. My background: [paste your CV or a summary] The role: [paste the job description] Interview me properly: 1. Ask the five hardest questions this specific role would produce — one at a time, wait for my answer 2. After each answer, tell me what was strong, what was vague, and what a sharper version sounds like 3. Push back once per question if my answer is thin Rules: - Don't move on until I've answered - Don't be gentle — being surprised in the real interview is worse - At the end, tell me the one weakness in my answers a real interviewer would notice

6Before the room

The email to someone who's ignored three already

The problem isn't persistence. It's sending the same email to someone who already decided not to answer it.

Prompt

You are an expert in persuasion. Write an email that gets a reply from someone who has ignored the previous ones. Who I'm emailing: [name, role, what you know about them] What I need from them: [the specific action or answer] What I've already sent: [summarise the previous emails and when] What they care about: [their priorities, what they're working on] Write one email that: 1. Opens with something specific to them, not a generic line 2. Makes replying feel easy rather than effortful 3. Gives them a reason to reply that's about them, not about me Rules: - No "I hope this finds you well". No explaining why I'm following up again - Under 100 words - End with one specific question they can answer in a single sentence

7Before the room

Ask for a raise with the case already built

Most people go in with a feeling. Go in with a document.

Prompt

You are a compensation advisor who has sat on both sides of this conversation. My role: [title, level, how long] What I've actually delivered: [specific outcomes, numbers where you have them] What I'm asking for: [figure or band] My manager: [how they decide, what constraints they're under] Build my case: 1. The three strongest pieces of evidence, ordered by how hard they are to argue with 2. How to open so it's a business conversation rather than a personal one 3. Their three most likely responses and my answer to each 4. What to say if the answer is "not right now" Rules: - No inflated language — everything must be defensible - End with the one number or fact I should make sure gets said out loud

8Before the room

Feedback you've been avoiding giving

You've rewritten it in your head six times and still haven't said it.

Prompt

You are an experienced manager known for being direct without being harsh. Who: [their role, how long they've been here, how they usually take feedback] The behaviour: [what they actually do — specific, observable, not a personality trait] The impact: [what it costs the team or the work] What I've tried: [anything you've already said] Give me: 1. The exact opening sentence 2. How to describe the behaviour with one concrete example, not a pattern claim 3. What good looks like, stated as something they can do on Monday 4. What to say if they get defensive, and what to say if they agree too quickly Rules: - No compliment sandwich - Nothing that could be read as a comment on who they are rather than what they did - End with the one sentence that should close the conversation

See what you can't see

09–16 · the ones that find your blind spot
9See what you can't

Pre-mortem: see it fail before it does

Post-mortems tell you why it died. Pre-mortems stop it dying.

Prompt

You are a strategist who specialises in failure analysis. The plan: [describe it in detail] The timeline: [when things happen] What I'm assuming will go right: [your assumptions] It is [six months] from now and this has failed completely. Work backwards: 1. The three most likely causes of death, ranked by probability not drama 2. For each, the earliest signal I could have noticed 3. The decision I make in the next two weeks that most determines which of these happens 4. What I'd wish I'd done differently Rules: - Assume failure. Don't hedge with "it might go well" - Be specific to this plan, not general project risk - End with the single cheapest thing I could do this week to reduce the biggest risk

10See what you can't

Find the assumption you haven't stated

The dangerous ones are never on your risk list — that's what makes them dangerous.

Prompt

The situation: [plan, decision or project, in as much detail as you can] What I'm confident about: [what you believe is solid] What I'm already uncertain about: [what you know might be wrong] Find what I can't see: 1. The assumptions I'm making that I haven't stated 2. What I'm taking for granted that might not be true 3. The blind spot most likely to cause problems 4. What someone who has watched this exact thing fail before would notice immediately Rules: - Do not cover the risks I already named — I've thought about those - Be specific to my situation, not generic - End with the single assumption that, if wrong, would do the most damage

11See what you can't

Trace the second- and third-order consequences

Most decisions are judged on what happens immediately. That's rarely where the cost shows up.

Prompt

You are a systems thinker. The decision: [what you're considering doing] The context it lands in: [team, market, constraints, who else is affected] Trace the consequences three levels deep: 1. First order — what happens immediately as a direct result 2. Second order — what happens because of those consequences 3. Third order — what happens after that For each level, separate the effects I'd expect from the ones I wouldn't. Rules: - Include effects on people who aren't in the decision but live with the result - Flag any second-order effect that would undo the benefit of the first - End with the consequence most likely to surprise me

12See what you can't

See what your writing actually says

You know what you meant. The reader only gets what you wrote.

Prompt

You are an expert in how written communication is received rather than intended. What I wrote: [paste it] Who reads it: [their role, their relationship to me, their mood coming in] What I intended: [what you were trying to convey] Tell me: 1. The tone they will actually perceive, not the one I intended 2. The specific line most likely to trigger a negative reaction, and why 3. What they'll assume about my motives from this 4. What to change, and the exact replacement wording Rules: - Read it as the recipient, not as an editor - Quote the exact lines you're reacting to - End with the one change that most improves how this lands

13See what you can't

The honest first impression nobody gives you

People are polite about your profile, your pitch, your homepage. That politeness costs you.

Prompt

You are [the exact person this is aimed at — role, seniority, what they're busy with]. You have never heard of me and you're giving this eight seconds. Here it is: [paste your bio / homepage / pitch / profile] Tell me honestly: 1. What you think I do, based only on this 2. Whether you'd keep reading, and the precise moment you'd stop 3. What impression you form of my competence and level 4. The one thing that makes you least likely to reply Rules: - Be blunt. Politeness here is a disservice - React as that person, not as an assistant assessing writing - End with the single change that would most improve your first impression

14See what you can't

The genuine devil's advocate

Ask "what do you think" and you get agreement. Ask for an attack and you get information.

Prompt

Here is what I believe: [your position, and why you hold it] Your job is to destroy it. Then rebuild it. 1. Attack — every assumption that could be wrong, every way this fails. Don't be polite, don't add qualifications, don't soften anything 2. Steelman — now build the strongest possible case that I'm right, stronger than I made it 3. Verdict — tell me what you actually think, and how confident you are Rules: - In part 1, argue as if you're being paid to win - Don't let part 2 quietly retract part 1 - End with the one piece of evidence that would settle this either way

15See what you can't

Find the actual cause, not the visible one

You keep fixing the thing that's obviously broken and it keeps breaking.

Prompt

The problem as I experience it: [what keeps happening] What I've already tried: [fixes and what happened] When it started, and what else changed around then: [timeline] Find the real cause: 1. Separate symptoms from causes in what I described 2. For the leading candidate, keep asking "and why does that happen" until you reach something structural rather than someone's mistake 3. Name what would have to be true for each candidate cause to be the real one 4. Tell me the cheapest test that would distinguish between them Rules: - Do not accept my framing of the problem if the evidence suggests another one - End with the test I should run first, and what result would mean what

16See what you can't

Turn a mess into a decision

You've been going round on this for a week and it isn't getting clearer by thinking harder.

Prompt

Here's the situation, unstructured: [dump everything, however messy] Turn this into a decision I can actually make: 1. What the real options are — including any I haven't named 2. What each one requires from me: money, time, risk, reputation 3. What I'd have to believe for each to be the right choice 4. What I'm actually optimising for, based on how I described this rather than what I claimed Rules: - Point out where I've described a preference as a constraint - Don't recommend yet — ask me the two questions that would settle it - End by naming the decision underneath the decision I said I was making

Think it through

17–24 · reasoning, not retrieval
17Think it through

Expose the skeleton of any argument

You can't be misled by something you can see completely.

Prompt

You are an expert in logic and critical thinking. The argument: [paste the article, claim or post] Expose its structure: 1. The main conclusion being argued for 2. The premises it depends on 3. The assumptions made but never stated 4. The weakest link in the chain 5. What evidence would actually be needed to establish it Rules: - Quote the exact lines you're analysing - Do not tell me whether you agree with the conclusion - End with the one question that most undermines this argument

18Think it through

Build the argument that's already in your notes

The idea isn't weak. The structure isn't there yet.

Prompt

You are an expert in argumentation. Your job is not to add ideas I don't have — it's to build the argument already inside what I've written. My notes, fragments and half-thoughts: [paste everything, however messy] What I'm trying to argue, in one imprecise sentence: [your core point] Who I'm trying to convince: [who reads this and what they already believe] Build it: 1. The clearest version of my core claim 2. The three strongest supporting points — drawn only from what I wrote 3. The order they should appear in, and why that order 4. What's missing that I need to go and find Rules: - Invent nothing. Use only what I gave you - Flag anywhere I contradict myself - End with my opening paragraph, written cleanly

19Think it through

Turn a book into a tool

A book is an argument, not an instruction. That's why you never apply them.

Prompt

You are an expert in applied learning. Your job is not to summarise this book — it's to extract its framework and apply it to me. The book: [title and author] My situation: [the problem, decision or goal you're facing] Do two things: 1. Extract the core framework — the one model the author is really teaching, stripped of stories and examples, under 200 words 2. Apply it to my situation — how it maps onto what I described, the specific insight it produces, and the action it points to Rules: - Do not summarise. Extract - Make the application specific to what I described - End with the one thing I should do differently this week because of it

20Think it through

Understand anything through analogy (Feynman)

Most explanations use the vocabulary of the thing you're trying to understand. You leave with a definition, not comprehension.

Prompt

Explain [topic] using only analogies and everyday examples. No jargon at all. Assume I have no background in this field. How to run it: 1. Give me one analogy, then stop 2. Ask me a question that tests whether I actually understood it 3. Based on my answer, go deeper or change the analogy 4. Keep going until I can explain it back to you in my own words with no technical terms Rules: - Never introduce a term without an analogy first - If my explanation back to you has a gap, say exactly where - End by telling me which part I found hardest, so I know what to revisit

21Think it through

Reverse-engineer why something worked

Copying the surface of something successful gets you the surface.

Prompt

You are an analyst who studies why things work rather than whether they did. The thing that worked: [paste the post, email, page, pitch or campaign] Context: [audience, where it appeared, what result it got] Take it apart: 1. The structural choice that made it effective 2. The psychological trigger it activated 3. The single line doing the most work, and why 4. What most people would have written instead, and why that fails Then rebuild it for my situation: [your topic and audience] Rules: - Don't describe what it says. Explain why that ordering and phrasing works - End with the one principle here that transfers to anything else I write

22Think it through

Map a tradeoff you keep flip-flopping on

You've argued both sides so many times you've lost track of which facts are real.

Prompt

The tradeoff: [A versus B] What I've told myself in favour of A: [reasons] In favour of B: [reasons] Constraints that are genuinely fixed: [money, time, people, deadlines] Sort this out: 1. Separate my reasons into facts, estimates, and preferences dressed up as facts 2. Identify which single variable actually drives the answer 3. Tell me at what value of that variable the answer flips 4. Say which side the evidence currently favours, and how strongly Rules: - Be explicit where I'm using a preference as though it were a constraint - End with the one piece of information that would end this debate

23Think it through

Argue the position you disagree with

You can't properly hold a view you've never seen defended well.

Prompt

The position I hold: [your view] The position I dismiss: [the opposing view] Why I dismiss it: [your reasons] Do this: 1. Make the strongest possible case for the position I dismiss — stronger than its actual advocates usually make 2. Identify which of my reasons for dismissing it is weakest, and why 3. Name what a reasonable, well-informed person would have to value differently to hold that view 4. Tell me which parts of my position survive this intact Rules: - Don't strawman either side - Don't conclude that both sides have a point unless the evidence genuinely says so - End with the specific thing I'd need to learn to hold my view more honestly

24Think it through

Sanity-check a number before you use it

One wrong figure in a deck outlives every good thing in it.

Prompt

The claim I'm about to make: [the number or statistic] Where it came from: [source, or how you calculated it] What I'm using it to argue: [the point it supports] Before I use this: 1. Check the arithmetic independently, showing your working 2. Tell me what this number does and does not actually measure 3. Identify the most likely way it could be technically true but misleading in my context 4. Say what a sceptical reader would ask about it first Rules: - Don't assume my source is right - If you can't verify it, say so plainly rather than reasoning around it - End with how I should caveat it if I use it anyway

Read hard things

25–30 · documents that were not written for you
25Read hard things

Understand what you're actually signing

The language was written to be read by lawyers, not by the person agreeing to it.

Prompt

You are a translator between legal language and plain English. You are not giving legal advice — you are telling me what this document says. The document: [paste it] My situation: [who you are in this, what you're agreeing to and why] Tell me: 1. What I'm committing to, in plain language, clause by clause where it matters 2. What the other side is committing to — and what they're carefully not committing to 3. Every clause that creates risk for me, ranked by how much 4. What's missing that I'd expect to see in a fair version of this 5. The specific things to ask them to change, with suggested wording Rules: - Quote the clause, then translate it - Flag anything genuinely ambiguous rather than picking a reading - End with the one clause I should not sign without professional advice

26Read hard things

Read a report for one specific decision

You don't need to understand the whole report. You need the part that changes what you do.

Prompt

The document: [paste or attach] The decision I'm making: [the actual choice in front of you] What I already know: [so it doesn't repeat it back] Read this for my decision, not in general: 1. What in here bears directly on my choice 2. What in here I can safely ignore, and why 3. Anything that contradicts what I told you I already know 4. What the document implies but doesn't say Rules: - Cite the section for each point so I can check it - Don't summarise the parts that don't matter to me - End with how this document should change my decision, if at all

27Read hard things

Compare two versions and find what moved

Someone sent back "a few small changes."

Prompt

Version A: [paste the original] Version B: [paste the revision] What matters to me: [the commitments, numbers or claims you care about] Compare them: 1. Every substantive change, ignoring pure wording and formatting 2. For each, what it changes in practice — not just what words moved 3. Anything removed entirely 4. Any change that looks cosmetic but shifts meaning or obligation Rules: - Ignore differences that have no practical effect - Put anything affecting what I listed as mattering at the top - End with the single change I'd most regret not noticing

28Read hard things

Find what a document is carefully not saying

The interesting part of a press release, a report or a proposal is usually the absence.

Prompt

The document: [paste it] Who wrote it and what they want: [the author and their incentive] Read it for the gaps: 1. What a complete version of this would include that this doesn't 2. Claims that are technically true but chosen to create a different impression 3. Numbers given without the denominator, comparison or timeframe that would make them meaningful 4. Questions this document makes it harder to ask Rules: - Distinguish between an omission that's normal for the format and one that's doing work - Don't speculate about motive beyond what the text supports - End with the one question I should put to the author

29Read hard things

Get through a pile of documents you inherited

Fifty files, a deadline, and no idea which four matter.

Prompt

I've uploaded [n] documents relating to [what]. What I'm trying to work out: [your actual question] Before I read any of them: 1. Tell me what's in this set — types, dates, what each covers in one line 2. Rank them by relevance to my question, and say why the top ones win 3. Flag any two that contradict each other, and which looks more authoritative 4. Tell me what's missing that I'd need to answer my question properly Rules: - Name files explicitly so I can find them - Don't summarise everything — I want a reading order, not a digest - End with the three I should read first and what to look for in each

30Read hard things

Translate jargon into something you can act on

You've read the paragraph four times and still can't tell what it wants you to do.

Prompt

The text: [paste it] My background: [what you do know / what field you're from] Why I'm reading it: [what you need to do with it] Translate: 1. What it actually says, in plain language, same order 2. Each piece of jargon defined the first time it appears — briefly, in context 3. What it wants me to do or decide, if anything 4. What I'd need to already know for this to have been readable Rules: - Don't oversimplify to the point of changing the meaning — flag where precision matters - Use my background as the starting point rather than assuming zero knowledge - End with the one sentence that carries the actual point

An ongoing partner

31–36 · the ones you come back to weekly
31Ongoing partner

The mentor who doesn't flatter you

Everyone around you is invested in your comfort. Nobody's telling you the thing.

Prompt

You are a mentor with thirty years in [my field]. You are respected because you're useful, not because you're kind. You've seen people with my profile succeed and fail and you know which signals predict which. Where I am: [role, level, how long, what you've actually achieved] Where I want to be: [goal and rough timeline] What I think is holding me back: [your theory] Give me: 1. Your honest assessment of the gap between where I am and where I say I'm going 2. Whether my theory about what's holding me back is right — and if not, what is 3. The one skill or behaviour that would move me furthest, and why that one 4. What I'm doing now that I should stop Rules: - No encouragement that isn't earned. No hedging - If my goal is unrealistic on that timeline, say so and say what is realistic - End with what I should do in the next thirty days

32Ongoing partner

Weekly review that finds patterns

You review the week, feel vaguely behind, and do the same week again.

Prompt

Here's my week: [what you did, what you didn't, what happened] What I said I'd do: [last week's intentions] How it felt: [energy, frustration, what dragged] Review it properly: 1. What actually got done versus what I planned, without softening the gap 2. Where the time went that I didn't account for 3. Any pattern here I've probably shown before — ask me if you need to check 4. The one thing that, if it had gone differently, would have changed the week most Rules: - Distinguish between "didn't have time" and "avoided it" - Don't produce a motivational summary - End with one specific change for next week, and how I'll know if it worked

33Ongoing partner

A learning coach that tests you

Reading feels like learning. It mostly isn't.

Prompt

I'm learning [subject] so I can [do what] by [when]. What I understand so far: [be honest] What confuses me: [the sticky bits] Be my coach: 1. Test what I claim to understand before we go further — questions, not explanations 2. When I'm wrong, don't give me the answer; ask a smaller question that gets me there 3. Build the next stage only on what I've demonstrated, not what I've read 4. Track what I keep getting wrong across our sessions Rules: - One question at a time, wait for my answer - Get harder as I get things right - End each session by telling me what to review and what we do next time

34Ongoing partner

Think out loud with something that pushes back

You don't need an answer. You need to hear yourself explain it to something that will interrupt.

Prompt

I want to think out loud about [topic]. I don't want a solution yet. How to be useful: 1. Let me talk. Ask a question when something I said doesn't follow, is vague, or contradicts something earlier 2. Reflect back what you're hearing underneath what I said, especially if it differs from what I claimed 3. Name the assumption I keep returning to without examining 4. Don't offer a conclusion until I ask for one Rules: - One question at a time - Short responses — you're a mirror with opinions, not a lecturer - When I finally ask, give me your honest read including anything I clearly don't want to hear

35Ongoing partner

Prepare for the week before it starts

Monday morning spent working out what Monday is for.

Prompt

Here's my week ahead: [meetings, deadlines, commitments] My actual priorities: [the two or three things that matter] What's carried over: [unfinished from last week] Set me up: 1. Sort what's urgent from what's important, and name anything that's neither 2. Sequence the week around dependencies — what has to happen before what 3. Flag every conflict between my stated priorities and my actual calendar 4. Identify what could be delegated, shortened, or cancelled outright Rules: - Be blunt about the mismatch between what I said matters and where the hours go - Don't fill the week — leave slack - End with the single thing that, if it's the only thing I do this week, makes it a good week

36Ongoing partner

Rehearse before you commit to anything

A generic pre-commitment check you can run on any decision.

Prompt

I'm about to commit to [decision]. Why I want to: [your reasons] What it costs: [money, time, opportunity] What I'd have to give up: [the tradeoff] Before I commit: 1. Ask me the three questions that would most change my answer 2. Tell me what I'd need to believe for this to be obviously right 3. Name the version of me in six months who regrets this, and what they'd say 4. Name the version who regrets not doing it, and what they'd say Rules: - Ask the questions first and wait — don't answer them for me - End by telling me whether this is reversible, and how much that should matter here

Build & reuse

37–40 · prompts that produce other prompts
37Build & reuse

Have Claude write the prompt for you

The most underused move there is. If you don't know how to ask, ask for the asking.

Prompt

I need help with this task: [describe what you're actually trying to do, in plain language] What a good result looks like: [how you'd know it worked] What I have available: [files, data, context you can supply] Write me the best possible prompt for this task. Include: 1. The role you should adopt 2. The context slots I need to fill in, with a note on what belongs in each 3. Exactly what output format to produce 4. The constraints that would most improve the result Then tell me what information I'd need to gather before running it. Rules: - Write the prompt for me to copy, not a description of a prompt - Flag anything you had to assume - End by asking me the one question whose answer would most improve it

38Build & reuse

Make it interview you before it starts

Three questions up front beats four rounds of revision after.

Prompt

I want you to [the task]. Don't start yet. First, interview me: 1. Ask me what you need to know to make this genuinely good rather than generic 2. One question at a time — wait for each answer 3. Stop asking when more questions would stop improving the result, and tell me you're stopping 4. Then summarise what you understood and what you're still assuming, and wait for me to confirm Rules: - Ask about my situation and constraints, not about things you could reasonably decide yourself - Maximum six questions - Don't produce any of the actual work until I've confirmed your summary

39Build & reuse

Turn something you do often into a skill

If you've explained the same process three times, stop explaining it.

Prompt

I want to turn [the recurring task] into a reusable skill. Interview me about how I actually do it: 1. Ask one question at a time about the steps, the inputs, and what makes the output good 2. Specifically ask about the parts I do without thinking — those are the ones I'll forget to mention 3. Ask what a bad version looks like, not just a good one 4. When you understand it, tell me what you'd put in the skill and what's still ambiguous — and wait before writing anything Rules: - Don't accept "it depends" — ask what it depends on - Include the checks I do at the end, not just the production steps - End by telling me the one part of my process that's hardest to encode, and why

40Build & reuse

Capture your writing voice permanently

Ask for "my style" without examples and you get its style with your topic.

Prompt

Here are [3–5] things I've written: [paste sample 1] [paste sample 2] [paste sample 3] Analyse my style — an analysis, not a description: 1. Sentence length and rhythm, with the actual numbers 2.
