Lesson 13 · Prompting habits · read in ~7 minutes

Where the
quality leaks

Claude isn't the problem. The gap between an average result and a great one is everything that happens around the model — before you hit send, after the first reply lands, and in the setup you never did. Twelve leaks, four places they spring.

Leak 01

How you prompt

The fix is never a longer prompt. It's a more complete one.

Mistake 01

Too vague — no context, no goal, no audience

A prompt that makes sense to you can mean almost nothing to Claude. It fills the blanks, and it fills them with the most generic version of what you asked for.

Without contextWrite a LinkedIn post about our new product launch.
With contextI'm the marketing lead at a B2B SaaS company. We're launching a project management tool aimed at remote engineering teams. Write a LinkedIn post announcing the launch — professional but not corporate, aimed at CTOs and engineering managers. The goal is waitlist sign-ups.

The fixThree things before anything else: who you are and what you're working on, what you want to achieve, and who the output is for.

Mistake 02

Asking three things in one prompt

Claude doesn't prioritise. Ask for three things and it splits attention evenly and does all three at 60%. You get a post with no hook, a summary with no numbers, and an email that could be from anyone.

The fixOne prompt, one task. Finish it, then start the next. Claude in a focused conversation is a different tool from Claude pulled three ways.

Mistake 03

No role before the task

Without a role, Claude picks the most average version of what you asked for. Adding "you are a senior copywriter with ten years of B2B experience" shifts five things at once: tone, vocabulary, what it assumes you already know, how far it pushes past the obvious, and how it structures the output.

The fixOne line at the top. It doesn't constrain Claude — it gives it a starting point that isn't the middle of everything.

Mistake 04

Not saying what format you want

Claude will always give you something. Left to choose, it picks whatever is most average — usually a wall of text with headers, nested bullets and a conclusion you didn't ask for. Reformatting it costs you the time you saved.

Without formatWrite me a competitive analysis of Notion vs Linear.
With formatWrite me a competitive analysis of Notion vs Linear. Give it to me as a simple table — rows for each criterion, two columns. No intro, no conclusion, just the table.

The fixOne extra sentence, before it starts.

Leak 02

What you do with the first reply

This is where most of the quality is actually lost — and it costs nothing to fix.

Mistake 05

Accepting the first draft

The first response is Claude's best guess at what "good" looks like for your request. Sometimes the guess is right. Usually there's a sharper version one message away.

The fixOne follow-up beats an hour of prompt engineering. Send it back — every time.

Mistake 06

Not saying what was wrong

When it misses, the instinct is to send the same request again, reworded. It almost never works — Claude doesn't know what you disliked, so it makes the same assumptions and lands in the same place.

Asking againCan you try that again?
Saying what missedClose, but the tone is too formal — make it more conversational. The second paragraph is too long, cut it in half. Lead with the benefit, not the feature. Keep everything else.

The fixName the edit, not the mood. Claude can't fix what it doesn't know is broken.

Mistake 07

Starting a new chat out of habit

A long conversation gets better as it goes: it picks up your tone, your standards, the decisions you've already made. Open a new tab out of reflex and you throw all of that away and start at the warm-up again.

The fixIf the work spans more than one sitting, stay in the thread — but read the correction below, because this rule has a real limit.

Correction · the source overstates this one

"Claude has no memory between conversations" is no longer true

The advice to stay in one thread is good. The reason given for it isn't — and two of your own source guides flatly contradict each other here, so it's worth settling.

  • Memory exists now. Claude carries your role, preferences and working style into new chats, and you can review, edit or delete any of it in Settings. Projects each keep their own separate memory too. Starting fresh no longer means starting from zero.
  • Long threads have a real cost. Everything earlier in a conversation stays loaded — so when you switch topics inside a long chat you pay for irrelevant context on every response, and old context can bleed into the new topic.
  • The honest rule: stay in the thread while the context still helps. Start fresh when you change topic, or when the conversation has gone off the rails — Lesson 03's advice. With Projects and Memory set up, restarting is cheap, which is exactly why the setup matters more than the habit.

Leak 03

Your setup

If you type the same context at the start of every session, that isn't a prompting habit. It's a setup problem.

Mistake 08

No standing instructions

Every conversation that opens with you explaining who you are and how you like things is a conversation where the first ten messages get you back to where you already were.

The fixWrite it once — your role, your preferences, your standards, what you never want. There's a fill-in template in the Vault.

Mistake 09

Using the wrong model for the task

Not everything needs the most capable model, and not everything should go to the fastest one. Run everything through the same model and some outputs feel shallow while others feel needlessly slow.

The fixMatch the model to what the task actually demands — speed for summarising and formatting, depth for reasoning and nuanced writing. Turn on extended thinking when the problem is genuinely hard, not by default.

Mistake 10

Not using Projects for recurring work

If you do the same kind of work more than once, re-explaining it each time is the whole cost. People who say Claude finally understands them didn't find a better prompt — they stopped starting from zero.

The fixWrite your context and instructions once inside a Project. Every conversation in it starts already loaded.

Correction · model names go stale fast

The principle holds. The list doesn't.

Guides that name specific models date badly — Anthropic ships new ones several times a year, and the tiers get renamed. Learn the shape rather than the labels.

  • Fast and light for summarising, classifying, formatting — anywhere speed beats depth.
  • The mid-tier default for most writing, research, analysis and coding. This is where you should spend most of your time.
  • The most capable tier for genuinely hard reasoning and nuanced work. Reaching for it by default costs you speed for no gain.
  • Check the current lineup in the model switcher itself, or at Anthropic's help centre. Any article naming specific versions is a snapshot, not a rule.
Leak 04

Your mindset

The last two aren't techniques. They're the reason the other ten happen.

Mistake 11

Treating it like Google

Google is for finding things that already exist. A search box takes a question; Claude takes a situation. Type a two-sentence query and you get a two-sentence answer — then conclude Claude is underwhelming and go back to Google.

The fixDon't ask for an answer. Give it the situation and work through it together. The people who get the most out of Claude stopped asking questions and started having conversations.

Mistake 12

Not using Claude to learn

Most people take the output and close the tab. The most underused move in the whole tool is asking it to explain what it did and why. The people who improve fastest aren't the ones who get the best first drafts — they're the ones who understand why something worked and carry it forward.

The fixAfter any output, ask: why did you structure it that way? What did you assume I wanted? What's the weakest part of this? What would an expert say is missing?

Worth knowing · this advice has a shelf life

The prescriptive half fades. The context half doesn't.

Mistakes 3 and 4 tell you to assign roles and dictate format. That's sound today. But newer, stronger models need less of that steering — and guides on the latest models say the opposite: give goals rather than steps, and don't micromanage the approach. Both are right for their moment.

The part that never goes out of date is context: who you are, what you're trying to achieve, who it's for, and what "good" looks like. No model will ever guess your situation. The scaffolding you build around a weaker model is what you can drop later; the context is what you keep forever.

The honest summary: none of these twelve are hard to fix. That's the frustrating part. The gap between average results and great ones isn't talent and isn't time — it's twelve small habits nobody laid out for you. Pick one and do it differently today.
