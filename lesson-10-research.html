Lesson 10 · Research · ~8 minutes, and one quiz

Send out
an investigator

Research turns Claude from a conversational assistant into a systematic investigator: it plans, runs many searches that build on each other, decides what to chase next, and comes back with a cited report. It takes minutes, not seconds — which is exactly why the prompt matters.

One thing the lesson doesn't tell you

Research is paid plans only — Pro, Max, Team or Enterprise — on web, desktop and mobile. It isn't on Free. Worth knowing before you go hunting the menu for it.

Part 01

Route the question

Five real requests. Four places each could go. Decide before you click — the answer appears the moment you choose, and guessing first is what makes the boundary stick.

Question 1 of 5

"Compare the three payroll providers we're considering — pricing, implementation time, and support quality — and give me sources I can check."

Research. Right call. A comparative analysis needing information from multiple sources, synthesised into one answer, with citations you can verify — all four signals at once. A single search can't compare three vendors on three criteria.

Not this one. One search returns one page. Three vendors judged on three criteria is nine cells to fill.

Not this one. There's nothing here to reason out — every fact you need is outside the question.

Not this one. Vendor pricing lives on the public web, not in your company's documents.

Question 2 of 5

"I'm prepping the quarterly investor update — what's our biggest competitor's current stock price?"

Understandable, but no. Research would spend minutes gathering breadth this question doesn't need. The size of the project isn't the size of the question.

Quick web search. Right call. The investor update sounds big, but the question itself is a single fact with one authoritative source. When you need a quick, specific answer and speed matters more than comprehensiveness, web search is the right size.

Not this one. No amount of reasoning produces today's share price.

Not this one. It's a public market figure, not something sitting in your company's files.

Question 3 of 5

"This SQL query keeps returning duplicate rows and I can't see why. Walk through the logic and find the bug."

Not this one. There's nothing to gather. The bug is already in the text you pasted.

Close, but no. You could look up how JOINs behave — but the fault is in this specific query, and no page on the internet has seen it.

Thinking. Right call. Everything needed to answer is already in the question — the answer comes from reasoning carefully through the query's logic, not from gathering outside information. Code debugging, maths and logical analysis are Thinking's home ground.

Not this one. Your colleagues haven't written up this particular bug.

Question 4 of 5

"Plan our 20-person team offsite: compare two or three city options with venues, rough costs, and travel logistics."

Research. Right call. Planning a complex project means pulling together many sources — venues, prices, logistics — and synthesising them into something you can act on. That's thorough-investigation territory: the kind of work that would otherwise take hours by hand.

Not this one. Venues, costs and travel across three cities is not one lookup.

Not this one. You can't reason your way to a venue's catering price.

Not this one. Unless your company owns the venues, all of this lives outside.

Question 5 of 5

"What's our travel reimbursement policy for international trips?"

Not this one. Research investigates the outside world. Your policy isn't out there.

Not this one. The public web has other companies' policies — not yours.

Not this one. There's nothing to deduce. The document either says it or it doesn't.

Enterprise search. Right call. The answer lives in your organisation's own documents, not on the public web. Company-specific questions are exactly what enterprise search draws from — Research investigates the outside world; your policies live inside.

Five of five. Right call every time.

That's the whole routing skill. Now the harder half — you've picked the right tool, but a Research run takes minutes, so the prompt you send it with decides whether those minutes were worth spending.

Part 02

Upgrade the prompt

Take the payroll comparison — the kind of question Research is for — and make it worth the run. Both versions in each pair are polished and about the same length. Only one does more work.

First move · the goal

Both are specific. Both ask for sources. Which steers the report better?

The other one does more work. This version aims at the topic. "The payroll provider market" gets you an industry overview — thorough, well-sourced, and not the thing you have to decide.

Right. Scope to your decision, not the topic. The first scopes to the payroll market, so Research writes an industry overview. This one scopes to the choice you're actually making: which provider fits a 200-person company, judged on the three criteria you'll weigh. A report can only aim at the decision if the prompt names it.

Second move · one more line

One line before you send it. Which addition does more work?

Right. Constrain the sources and the timeframe. Naming which sources count and how recent they must be changes what Research actually gathers: current, verifiable pricing instead of stale summaries.

It reads thorough — that's the trap. None of history, leadership or reputation feeds the decision, and breadth you won't use dilutes the report you will. More sections isn't more signal.

The four doors, in full

The quiz taught the boundaries by example. Here they are as a list you can come back to.

Door 01

Use Research when

  • You need a comprehensive report that synthesises multiple sources
  • You want in-depth analysis across the web and your connected integrations
  • The investigation would otherwise take hours of manual work
  • It's comparative — competitors, vendor options, alternatives
  • You need citations you can actually verify
Door 02

Use web search when

  • You need one quick, specific fact — a stock price, an address
  • One or two sources will settle it
  • Speed matters more than comprehensiveness
Door 03

Use Thinking when

  • The problem is complex but needs no outside information
  • It's maths, code debugging or logical analysis
  • The answer comes from reasoning through, not gathering up
Door 04

Use enterprise search when

  • The answer is in your organisation's documents, Slack threads, emails or meeting notes
  • You're onboarding and need to know how your company does something
  • The question is company-specific, not about the public web
Research is ideal for tasks like
  • Market analysis and competitive research
  • Planning complex projects — team offsites, product launches
  • Synthesising your email, calendar and documents
  • Technical documentation drawing on several sources
  • Briefings that need current, verified information
Part 03

What happens while you wait

Not one long search. A tree that grows as it goes — Claude decides what to look at next based on what the last look turned up, which is why it takes minutes and why it can cover ground you'd never have thought to name.

Step 01

It plans first

Claude thinks through its approach before searching anything: breaks down your request, works out what information it needs, and plans how to investigate each angle.

Step 02

It searches, then re-decides

Many searches that build on each other. What it finds determines what it looks for next — chasing promising leads, filling gaps you never specified.

Step 03

It synthesises

Everything gathered — from the web and any connected integrations like Gmail, Calendar or Drive — is compiled into one organised report.

Step 04

It cites

Every claim links back to its source, so verifying a number or digging further takes one click rather than a second investigation.

Worth knowing, and not in the lesson

Those branches are separate agents, running at the same time

Anthropic's engineering team has described Research as a multi-agent system: a lead agent plans the approach, then spawns parallel subagents that search simultaneously, each with its own context. That's why a run takes minutes and can touch hundreds of sources — and it explains exactly where Research beats everything else. Their evaluations found the multi-agent setup excels on breadth-first questions: the ones with several independent directions worth pursuing at once.

The practical upshot: when you write the prompt, name the independent angles. "Pricing, implementation time and support quality" isn't just clearer — it's three directions that can be chased in parallel.

Source: Anthropic Engineering, "How we built our multi-agent research system".

Part 04

Running one well

Four clicks to start it. The rest of this section is about the prompt — because a run takes minutes rather than seconds, time spent on the prompt is the cheapest time in the whole exercise.

01
Click the + buttonBottom left of your chat interface.
02
Select ResearchIt highlights once active — you'll see an indicator at the bottom of the chat window.
03
Enter your prompt and submitThis is the part worth slowing down for.
04
Leave it runningClaude works in the background with progress indicators as it searches and analyses. Go and do something else.

Prerequisite: web search must be turned on for Research to function at all. If you haven't enabled it, it's in the same + menu. This is the single most common reason the option seems to do nothing.

Tip 01

Be specific about the goal

Name the decision, not the subject.

Instead of"Tell me about the EV market"
Try"Analyse the electric vehicle battery market — identify key players, technology trends, and supply chain challenges that might affect investment decisions."
Tip 02

Ask for the sections you want

Claude organises its findings around whatever structure you give it. "Compare venue options for a team offsite including: location and accessibility, meeting space and amenities, catering options, and pricing considerations." Name the sections and you get the report you can actually use.

Tip 03

Include your constraints

Budget ranges, timelines, geographic requirements, company size. Every constraint narrows what counts as a relevant option — and narrowing is what turns an overview into an answer.

Tip 04

Ask Claude to write the prompt

If you're not sure how to frame the question, ask Claude to help you write a better Research prompt before you enable the feature. Two minutes in a normal chat beats a ten-minute run pointed at the wrong thing.

With your connected tools

With Google Workspace or other integrations connected, Research pulls from your emails, calendar and documents alongside the web — which is where it stops being a search tool and starts being a briefing service.

  • "Summarise what's been discussed about Project X across my emails and Slack, then research industry best practices for similar initiatives."
  • "Review my calendar commitments for next week and research each company I'm meeting with."
  • "Find all internal documents about our pricing strategy and compare to how competitors are positioning themselves."

You can steer it mid-request too: "Pull relevant context from my Google Drive" or "Include insights from my recent emails on this topic."

The one habit that pays: before you send a Research prompt, read it back and ask what decision it's aimed at. If you can't name one, you'll get an overview — and you'll have waited ten minutes for it.
