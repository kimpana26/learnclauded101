Fifty real situations, each with the prompt most people type and the prompt that actually works. Don't read this as a catalogue. Go down the left column and find the three that are things you actually type — those are your habits, and fixing three beats skimming fifty. Nothing here is clever — every good version just says out loud what the bad one left in your head.

If you only remember three things
  1. Set the stage. Who you are, who it's for, what's already happened.
  2. Define the task. A verb, and the actual thing you want back.
  3. Specify the rules. Length, tone, format, and what to leave out.

Email & messages

01–05 · the ones you write every week
01Email

Telling a client the deadline slipped

Instead of

"Write an email about the project delay"

Try this

Write an email to [client name], our enterprise customer, explaining that [the integration] will be delayed by [two weeks]. This is the second delay and they've been patient so far. Lead with the new date, keep it professional but genuinely apologetic, and end with one concrete thing we're doing to prevent a third. Under 150 words.

Why It names the reader, the history, the tone and the length — four facts you already had and hadn't written down.

02Email

Saying no without burning the bridge

Instead of

"Help me decline this request politely"

Try this

I need to decline [request] from [who they are to me]. I want to keep the relationship warm because [reason]. Write three versions: a clean no, a no-with-an-alternative, and a not-right-now. Under 80 words each. Don't over-apologise — one apology maximum per version.

Why Asking for three options costs nothing and lets you pick by feel instead of re-prompting.

03Email

Chasing someone who hasn't replied

Instead of

"Write a follow up email"

Try this

I emailed [name] on [date] about [topic] and heard nothing. This is my second nudge. Write something short that gives them an easy way out, doesn't guilt them, and turns the ask into a yes/no question they can answer in one line. Three sentences maximum.

Why "Turn the ask into a yes/no question" is the move that actually gets replies.

04Email

Bad news to your own team

Instead of

"Tell my team about the budget cut"

Try this

Write a message to my [8-person design team] about [a 20% budget cut] taking effect [next quarter]. They will immediately worry about [headcount] — address that directly rather than talking around it. Cover what I know, what I don't know yet, and when I'll update them. Plain language, no corporate softening.

Why Naming the fear your audience actually has is what separates a real message from a memo.

05Email

Introducing two people

Instead of

"Write an intro email"

Try this

Write an introduction connecting [person A — role, why they're interesting] with [person B — role, why they're interesting]. Give one line on why each should care about the other, suggest a concrete first step, then get out of the way. Under 120 words, warm but not gushing.

Why A good intro does the thinking for both parties. That only works if you supply both sides.

Meetings & notes

06–10 · before, during and after the calendar invite
06Meetings

Turning a transcript into something useful

Instead of

"Summarize this meeting"

Try this

Here's the transcript from [meeting]. Give me four sections: (1) decisions actually made, (2) open questions with who owns each, (3) action items with owner and date, (4) anything someone volunteered to do that nobody wrote down. Ignore the small talk and don't invent owners — mark them unassigned if it wasn't said.

Why Section four is the one that saves you, and no default summary will ever include it.

07Meetings

Preparing for a 1:1 you're nervous about

Instead of

"Help me prepare for my 1:1"

Try this

I have a 1:1 with [my manager] on [day]. My goals this quarter are [X]. I want to raise [topic] but I'm worried it will sound like complaining. Help me plan: what to open with, how to frame that topic as a problem to solve rather than a grievance, and two questions I should ask them.

Why Say the anxiety out loud and Claude can solve for it. Leave it out and you get generic advice.

08Meetings

Building an agenda that cuts things

Instead of

"Make an agenda for this meeting"

Try this

Here are my notes for [meeting] with [attendees]. Build a [45]-minute agenda: minutes allocated per item, decision items before update items, and a clear owner for each. Then flag anything on this list that doesn't need a meeting at all and could be an email.

Why The last sentence is the valuable one — you asked it to shorten your day, not just format it.

09Meetings

Catching up after time off

Instead of

"What did I miss?"

Try this

I was out from [date] to [date]. From my connected threads and documents, tell me: decisions made that affect [my project], anything with my name on it, and the two or three things I have to act on this week. Skip anything already resolved without me.

Why Filters by what matters to you rather than by what happened most recently.

10Meetings

A post-mortem that finds real causes

Instead of

"Write a post mortem for this project"

Try this

Here's what happened on [project]. Write a blameless post-mortem: timeline, what went well, what went wrong, root causes, and three specific changes. For each root cause, keep asking "and why did that happen" until you reach something structural rather than a person's mistake. Name no individuals.

Why You specified the method, not just the template. That's what stops it stopping at symptoms.

Documents & decks

11–15 · things other people have to read
11Docs & decks

A deck from a pile of notes

Instead of

"Make a presentation about our Q3 results"

Try this

Turn these notes into a [10]-slide deck for [our board]. One idea per slide. Titles must state the takeaway, not the topic — "Churn is concentrated in one segment", never "Churn analysis". Speaker notes under each. Slide 1 is the single thing I want them to remember if they forget everything else.

Why Takeaway titles are the difference between a deck that argues and a deck that lists.

12Docs & decks

Cutting something in half

Instead of

"Make this shorter"

Try this

Cut this [two-page memo] to [one page]. Keep every number and every commitment I've made. Remove throat-clearing, hedging, and anything that repeats a point already made. At the end, list what you cut so I can object to any of it.

Why Saying what must survive is more useful than saying how short. The cut-list keeps you in control.

13Docs & decks

A template you'll use fifty times

Instead of

"Write a project brief"

Try this

Write a reusable project brief template for [my team]. Include the sections we always end up arguing about later: success criteria, who decides, what's explicitly out of scope. Use square-bracket placeholders I fill in each time, and put a one-line note under each heading explaining what belongs there.

Why Building for the fiftieth use rather than the first is the whole idea behind Skills.

14Docs & decks

One piece, three channels

Instead of

"Make social posts from this"

Try this

Take this [blog post] and adapt it for three places: LinkedIn (professional, ~200 words, one insight), a short thread (punchy, 5 posts), and an internal announcement (casual, 3 sentences). The core claim must stay identical in all three — change only register and length, never the substance.

Why Naming what must stay constant is what stops three versions becoming three different arguments.

15Docs & decks

An executive summary that survives skimming

Instead of

"Summarize this report"

Try this

Write a 150-word executive summary of this report for [a CFO with 90 seconds]. Lead with the recommendation. Then the two numbers that justify it. Then the biggest risk. No background section, no methodology, no throat-clearing.

Why Forbidding the sections they'd skip is faster than describing the ones they'd read.

Data & spreadsheets

16–20 · numbers you have to trust
16Data

Making sense of a dataset

Instead of

"Analyze this data"

Try this

Here's [our monthly sales data for two years]. Tell me the three clearest trends, anything that looks like a data error rather than a real signal, and the one question this raises that I should investigate next. Show your working for any number you quote so I can check it.

Why "Error rather than signal" catches the thing that would otherwise become a slide.

17Data

The spreadsheet you inherited

Instead of

"Explain this spreadsheet"

Try this

I inherited this workbook and don't know how it works. Map it: what each tab does, which cells are inputs versus calculations, how dependencies flow between sheets, and — most importantly — anything fragile that would silently break if someone typed over it.

Why The fragility is what bites you. Nobody thinks to ask for it.

18Data

Building a model you can defend

Instead of

"Build a financial model"

Try this

Build a [three-year revenue model] for [a 200-person B2B SaaS company]. Put every assumption in one clearly labelled tab and have all formulas reference it — never hard-code a number inside a formula. Add a scenario toggle for conservative / base / aggressive. Then list every assumption you made that I should challenge.

Why The last sentence turns a black box into something you can actually argue with.

19Data

Choosing the right chart

Instead of

"Make a chart of this"

Try this

I want to show [that churn is concentrated in one customer segment] to [a non-technical audience]. Suggest the chart type that makes that point fastest and explain why, then build it. If the data doesn't actually support that claim, tell me instead of drawing it anyway.

Why That final clause is a safeguard against making a beautiful chart of a wrong idea.

20Data

Checking someone else's numbers

Instead of

"Is this analysis right?"

Try this

Check this analysis against the raw data. Recompute each headline figure independently rather than following their method, flag any that don't reconcile, and tell me which conclusions would change if the discrepancies are real. Don't assume the original is correct.

Why "Recompute independently" is a different instruction from "review", and gets a different answer.

Research & competitive

21–25 · worth turning Research mode on for
21Research

Comparing vendors properly

Instead of

"Compare these vendors"

Try this

Compare [vendor A, B and C] for [a 200-person company choosing a payroll provider]. Judge on pricing, implementation time and support quality only. Prioritise vendor pricing pages and customer reviews from the last twelve months. Include sources I can check. End with which you'd pick and what evidence would change your mind.

Why Scoped to the decision, not the topic — and the source constraint keeps it from quoting stale summaries.

22Research

Understanding a market

Instead of

"Tell me about the EV market"

Try this

Analyse the [electric vehicle battery] market: key players, technology trends, and supply chain challenges that might affect [an investment decision in the next 18 months]. Cite sources. Where the evidence is thin or contested, say so rather than filling the gap with a confident sentence.

Why Explicitly inviting "I don't know" is the cheapest defence against confident-sounding nonsense.

23Research

Prepping for a meeting with a company

Instead of

"Research this company"

Try this

I'm meeting [company] on [date] to discuss [what]. Research: recent news, anything they've publicly said about [our problem space], who's likely in the room and what each of them cares about, and three questions that would show I've done my homework. Also flag anything I should avoid raising.

Why Asking for the landmines is as valuable as asking for the talking points.

24Research

Planning a literature review

Instead of

"Help me with my literature review"

Try this

I'm reviewing the literature on [topic] for [a thesis chapter]. Before I read anything, help me plan: the main schools of thought, the seminal papers, where the live disagreements are, and search terms I'd probably miss. Tell me what order to read in and why that order.

Why Planning the search beats running a bad one and reading two hundred abstracts.

25Research

Checking a claim you've seen repeated

Instead of

"Is this true?"

Try this

Check this claim: [paste the claim]. Find the primary source if one exists. Tell me what it actually says versus how it's being reported, who benefits from the claim being repeated, and give me a confidence level with your reasoning for it.

Why Separating the source from the reporting of it is where most viral claims fall apart.

Thinking & decisions

26–30 · where a thinking partner beats a search box
26Thinking

Stress-testing a plan

Instead of

"What do you think of my plan?"

Try this

Here's my plan: [plan]. Your job is to destroy it. Find every assumption I'm making that could be wrong and every way this fails. Don't be polite and don't add qualifications. Then steelman it — build the strongest case that I'm right. Then tell me what you actually think and name the single biggest risk.

Why Ask "what do you think" and you get agreement. Ask for an attack and you get information.

27Thinking

Finding the blind spot

Instead of

"Any risks I'm missing?"

Try this

Here's my situation: [situation]. I'm confident about [A]. I'm already worried about [B]. Don't cover either of those. Tell me what someone who has watched this fail before would notice immediately, and which unstated assumption would do the most damage if it turned out to be wrong.

Why Ruling out what you already know forces it past the obvious answers.

28Thinking

A decision you keep going round on

Instead of

"Should I take this job?"

Try this

I'm deciding between [option A] and [option B]. What matters to me: [list]. Don't recommend anything yet. First ask me the questions that would actually change my answer, one at a time. Then reflect back what you're hearing underneath what I said. Only then give your honest view — including anything I might not want to hear.

Why Sequencing the conversation means the advice arrives after the understanding, not before it.

29Thinking

Seeing through an argument

Instead of

"Summarize this article"

Try this

Expose the skeleton of this argument: the main conclusion, the premises it rests on, the assumptions it never states, the weakest link, and what evidence would actually settle it. Quote the exact lines you're analysing. Don't tell me whether you agree — end with the one question that most undermines it.

Why Withholding the verdict keeps you doing the judging, which is the point.

30Thinking

Untangling your own half-formed idea

Instead of

"Help me organize my thoughts"

Try this

Here are my messy notes on [topic]. Build the argument that's already inside them — do not add ideas I don't have. Give me: the clearest version of my core claim, the three strongest supporting points drawn only from what I wrote, the order they should appear in, and what's missing. Flag anything where I contradict myself.

Why "Don't add ideas I don't have" is what keeps the finished piece recognisably yours.

Learning something new

31–35 · the ones that build skill rather than deliver answers
31Learning

Understanding a concept properly

Instead of

"Explain [topic]"

Try this

Explain [topic] to me. I already understand [related thing], so build from there. Use one concrete example all the way through rather than a new one for each point. After each section, stop and ask me a question that checks whether I've actually understood it before continuing.

Why Anchoring to what you know plus a check question turns reading into learning.

32Learning

Getting something out of a book

Instead of

"Summarize this book"

Try this

Don't summarise [book title and author]. Extract the one framework the author is really teaching, stripped of stories and examples, in under 200 words. Then apply it to my situation: [situation]. End with the single thing I should do differently because of it.

Why A summary you forget; a framework applied to your own problem, you keep.

33Learning

Getting up to speed in a new field

Instead of

"Teach me about [field]"

Try this

I need working knowledge of [field] within [two weeks] so I can [do what]. Give me a learning path: what to learn first because everything else depends on it, what I can safely skip for now, the vocabulary I need to not sound lost, and a way to test myself at the end of each stage.

Why "What can I skip" is the question that makes a syllabus into a plan.

34Learning

Practising until it sticks

Instead of

"Quiz me on this"

Try this

Quiz me on [topic]. One question at a time and wait for my answer. If I'm wrong, don't give me the answer — ask a smaller question that leads me to it. Get harder as I get things right. At the end, tell me what I kept missing and what to review.

Why "Don't give me the answer" is the instruction that turns testing into tutoring.

35Learning

Explaining it to someone else

Instead of

"How do I explain this simply?"

Try this

I need to explain [topic] to [a non-technical stakeholder who mainly cares about cost]. Give me: one analogy that survives being poked at, the two things they will most likely misunderstand, and how to answer "so why does this cost so much?". Keep the whole thing under two minutes spoken.

Why Designing for the specific objection beats designing for "simple".

Projects & instructions

36–40 · set it up once, benefit forever
36Projects

Writing project instructions

Instead of

"Remember that I work in marketing"

Try this

Write project instructions for this workspace. I'm [role] at [company type] and we produce [what]. Always: [rules]. Never: [rules]. When I upload [type of file], automatically [do what]. Write it in second person as rules for you to follow, under 400 words, no generic advice.

Why Instructions are a program, not a fact about you. Write them as commands.

37Projects

Auditing a knowledge base before you trust it

Instead of

"Here are my files, let's start"

Try this

I've uploaded [n] documents about [topic]. Before we do anything: tell me what's actually in here, what's missing that you'd expect to see, where any two documents contradict each other, and which one I should treat as the source of truth when they conflict.

Why Finding the contradictions on day one is much cheaper than finding them in a deliverable.

38Projects

Automating a routine with instructions

Instead of

"Summarize these meeting notes" (every single time)

Try this

Add this to the project instructions: whenever I paste a meeting transcript, produce a summary in exactly this format — Decisions / Owners and dates / Open questions / One-line status for the weekly update. Never ask me to confirm the format again, just produce it.

Why Anything you've asked for three times should stop being a request and become a rule.

39Projects

Naming files so they can be found

Instead of

"Organize my files"

Try this

Look at these filenames. Propose a naming convention that makes them searchable — date format, project prefix, how to handle versions — and explain the reasoning in two lines. Then rename everything to match and show me a before/after list so I can veto any of them.

Why Propose, then apply, then let me veto. That sequence works for almost any bulk change.

40Projects

Keeping a workspace from going stale

Instead of

"Update the project"

Try this

Review this project's knowledge base against [the current quarter]. Tell me which documents are stale, which have been superseded by newer ones in here, and what's missing that I'd need for [upcoming work]. Don't delete or change anything — just give me the list and your reasoning.

Why Audit first, act second. Outdated documents quietly produce outdated answers.

Skills & repeatable work

41–45 · teaching Claude how you do things
41Skills

Creating a skill by being interviewed

Instead of

"Create a skill for writing reports"

Try this

I want to build a skill for [writing quarterly business reviews]. Interview me about how I do it now — one question at a time, don't batch them. When you think you understand it, tell me what you'd put in the skill and what's still ambiguous, and wait for me to confirm before you write anything.

Why The checkpoint before generation is what stops you getting a confident skill built on a guess.

42Skills

Capturing how you write

Instead of

"Write this in my style"

Try this

Here are [five things I've written]. Analyse my style in detail: sentence length, rhythm, vocabulary choices, how I open and close paragraphs, what I avoid, and any patterns that make it recognisably mine. Then write [new piece] matching it exactly. Do not fall back on your own default patterns.

Why Analysis first, then writing. Asking for "my style" without examples asks it to guess.

43Skills

Turning a process into a checklist

Instead of

"Make a checklist for this"

Try this

Turn our [contract review] process into a repeatable checklist. Here's how we do it: [steps]. Make every item a yes/no question rather than a topic, and include the steps people quietly skip when they're rushed. Put the irreversible ones first.

Why Yes/no items are the difference between a checklist and a list of things to think about.

44Skills

Fixing a skill that's misbehaving

Instead of

"Fix my skill, it's not working"

Try this

Here's my [brand guidelines] skill. It's producing [describe the problem]. Diagnose why first — is the instruction ambiguous, missing, or being overridden by something else? Then propose the smallest change that fixes it, and show me the before/after of just that section.

Why "Smallest change" stops it quietly rewriting the parts that were working fine.

45Skills

Standardising what your team produces

Instead of

"Make our reports more consistent"

Try this

Here are [six status reports] written by different people on my team. Work out what the good ones do that the weak ones don't. Turn that into a standard, written as instructions someone could follow without me explaining it. Include one worked example. Don't invent best practice — derive it from these.

Why Deriving the standard from your own good work beats importing someone else's.

Career & hard conversations

46–50 · the ones that matter most and get prompted worst
46Career

Raising something difficult

Instead of

"Help me talk to my boss about this"

Try this

I need to raise [issue] with [person]. Our relationship is [describe it]. Their most likely objection is [X]. Help me plan: how to open so they don't get defensive, the one sentence that states my ask plainly, and what to say if they respond with [likely bad reaction].

Why Rehearsing the failure mode is the whole value. Everything else you could improvise.

47Career

Feedback you've been putting off

Short version — the full version is in Vol 2

Instead of

"How do I give constructive feedback?"

Try this

I need to tell [a direct report] that [specific behaviour] is a problem. Context: they're [new / sensitive / more senior than me]. Draft what I'd actually say out loud: name the behaviour with a concrete example, the impact it had, and what good looks like instead. No compliment sandwich.

Why Banning the sandwich forces the message to survive on its own clarity.

48Career

A CV aimed at one job

Instead of

"Improve my resume"

Try this

Here's my CV and the job description I'm targeting. Before rewriting anything, tell me: which of my experiences genuinely map to their requirements and which don't, what I've buried that should be near the top, and what's missing that they will notice. Then rewrite the top third only.

Why Diagnosis before surgery — and scoping the rewrite keeps the rest recognisably yours.

49Career

Interview practice that actually helps

Instead of

"What questions will they ask me?"

Try this

I'm interviewing for [role] at [company]. Here's my background: [paste]. Ask me the five hardest questions they're likely to ask — one at a time, wait for my answer. After each, tell me what was strong, what was vague, and what a sharper version would sound like.

Why A list of questions teaches you nothing. Answering badly and being told why teaches you a lot.

50Career

Walking into a professional meeting as an equal

Short version — the full role-and-rules version is in Vol 2

Instead of

"What should I ask my doctor / lawyer / advisor?"

Try this

I'm meeting [a specialist] about [situation]. Here's what I've been told so far: [what you know]. Prepare me to walk in as an equal: the questions an informed person would ask, the red flags that should make me push back, what they might gloss over, and what I need to walk out knowing.

Why It shifts you from passive recipient to participant — the single highest-leverage prompt on this page.
